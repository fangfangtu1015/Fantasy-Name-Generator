Buried Places Are Named by the People Above Them

A site sheet for prep: who calls it what, the entry terms, the last party's fate, the hazard the name is actually describing, and the way out nobody has tested.

Five Groups Name a Buried Place, and Each One Lies Differently

A hole in the ground does not have a name. It has as many names as there are people with a reason to talk about it, and that is the single most useful fact for naming dungeons. Decide who is doing the naming and the shape of the name follows automatically, along with what the party can believe about the dangers inside.

Namer Name shape What it is really saying How reliable the map is
Neighbours Condition plus structure, no owner What they have seen from the top: sound, smell, weather, one body Accurate about the entrance, useless about anything below it
Antiquaries Structure of an entity, dates, citations Who built it and what it was for, argued from a doorway Good on purpose, wrong on what is down there now
A licensed company Owner plus numbering Someone has a deed, a fee schedule and an insurance problem Excellent where the ropes are, fictional where the money is
Survivors An event, phrased like a sentence Something happened to a specific person, and it is the whole name now Vivid, narrow, and usually about one room
Original builders Functional words in a dead language A work order: storage, tomb, water works, quarry The only names that describe the place as it was designed, which is not as it is
Run two names at once. The most economical trick here: give a site a scholarly name that appears on a purchased map and a village name that appears when you ask at the tavern. The mismatch tells the party, without a single dice roll, that the people who sold them the map have not been down there.

Structure Words Decide What Lives There

The noun you pick is a promise about content, and players who have run games for years will cash it in immediately. A crypt invites funerary monsters and cursed grave goods. A mine invites collapse, gas, and something that was already working when everyone fled. Pick the class first, then the modifier, and the site designs itself.

Class Words Plausible residents Plausible treasure
Funerary crypt, barrow, ossuary, charnel house, tomb, relief chamber Undead, guardians bound by oath, mourners who never left Grave goods, inscribed objects, family seals, nothing liquid
Worked ground mine, shaft, delving, quarry, sump, adit, level Things that eat rock or light, cave fauna, the abandoned crew Ore, tools, unpaid wages, a sealed seam nobody is told about
Civic underbelly sewer, cistern, culvert, undercroft, vault, stock tunnel Slime and life, refugees, the city's own dumped problems What the city threw away, and what it lost while doing so
Religious sanctum, reliquary, atrium, oratory, cloister, tithe cellar Clerics who stayed, vows given flesh, an order that failed Relics with paperwork, plate, indulgence ledgers
Natural throat, sink, swallet, geode, karst, fissure Animals, not builders: things that nest, hunt or hibernate Whatever fell in, and nothing that was placed
Ruined stair, cellar, underkeep, submerged hall, fall Whoever is squatting, whoever is hiding, whoever is stuck Salvage, and a story about the day the roof came down

Why "Natural" Names Are the Hardest to Sell

A natural site has no architect, so no one can honestly claim to know what is in it, and no treasure can be implied. That is a feature: a cave named for a geological action, with no owner and no builder, reads as the one place on the map where your clues stop working. If you want the players to feel genuinely uninformed, remove the possessive from the name.

The Name Changes as the Site Is Cleared

Places acquire and lose names as their reputation moves. Track the stage and you get both a name and a price, and the party learns the site's history from paperwork instead of a monologue.

  1. Rumour stage. Named by fear and by an event: the Weeping Stair, Where the Lamp Went Out. Nobody claims to have reached the bottom, so the name is a warning and the entrance is free.
  2. Company stage. Somebody has a rope, a licence and a debt: named for an owner and numbered by level. A fee appears, a guide appears, and the map stops being honest about the deep parts.
  3. Registered stage. The site is entered in an office as a lot, a ward or a claim number. Now the local event name survives only in the tavern, and the office name survives on receipts.
  4. Picked-clean stage. It is "the empty delving", "the flooded cellar", a shortcut. Danger has left, ownership has not, and the interesting conflict becomes who profits from a place that no longer has anything in it.

The Clearance stage control on this page produces the naming for each phase, because the same site at stage one and stage three should not be called the same thing.

Two Words on the Map, One Sentence in the Journal

A site usually carries three names at once and each has a length limit. Keep them separate and the place becomes usable:

Map label

One or two words, no possessive if the owner is disputed. On parchment, "Barrow Nine" beats "The Barrow of the Ninth Voice" every time, because the label has to fit between a river and a road.

Spoken name

Whatever locals shorten it to, and it will be shorter than the label. This is the form that appears in an interrogation, a joke or an order, so it must be sayable in one breath.

Record name

Full, dated, and attributed: the entry a scholar, a guild office or a temple would file. Long is fine here, because nobody reads it aloud.

Adventure title

Not an in-world name at all. A title is a promise to the player, so it may say too much: the in-world record says "Vault 12", the title says "The Thing in Vault 12 Came Out".

If you take one thing from this page, take this: generate the in-world name, then write a different title for the session, and let the gap between them be the joke or the dread.

How the Party Learns the Name on the Way There

A site reaches a table through four sources in a fixed order, and each one hands over a different form of the same name. Run them in sequence and the players learn the dungeon's whole history from paperwork, without you describing a single corridor. The trick is not to give them the name; it is to give them four incompatible names and let them notice.

Beat Who speaks Name form they use What they add or subtract Price
1. At the bar Neighbours, from above Condition plus structure, or an event sentence Adds one true sensory detail from the mouth and a warning that is really about a person, not the site A drink, and a name they will not repeat outside
2. In the office Clerk, registrar, surveyor Lot, claim or parcel number Subtracts every warning and adds a liability clause; the map here is neat and unvisited A search fee, and a copy drawn by someone who has not been
3. From the guide A licensed company's employee Owner plus numbering, with rope terminology Adds accurate detail down to the depth they have personally worked and nothing below it Hire, and a percentage of whatever comes up
4. At the mouth Carved, painted or scratched marks The builders' functional term, or a survivor's tally Adds the only version nobody contradicts, because it was written by someone who died there Nothing. It is the reason to have bought nothing else

Running the Sequence at the Table

Give beats one and two in the same session, deliberately: hand over the office map with its numbered lots while the bar name is still in the players' notes. Within two minutes somebody asks why the documents disagree, and from then on the players are investigating the site rather than entering it. At beat three, let the guide use the company's numbering fluently and stumble the moment a player describes something that matches the bar version; that is the entire performance. At beat four, allow one carved mark that supports the oldest version of all, and the party will begin to suspect that the scholarly attribution, the office number and the company's claim are all recent inventions sitting on top of something that was named for a work order.

One rule for the rooms below. Room names inside a site should come from a different hand than the site name. A place called by an office number should have chambers named by whoever worked, buried or worshipped there, because no clerk invents a room name. If your rooms carry numbered labels and the site carries an event name, that tells the party a company got here first, whatever the tavern says.

What the Site Was Before It Was a Dungeon

Nearly every buried place in a campaign was built for something else. A temple becomes a sewer, a mine becomes a refuge, a granary becomes a hermitage and then a lair. Archaeologists call this secondary use, and it is the most reliable source of a strong dungeon name because it hands you two naming layers at once: the word the builders used and the word the later users used. The friction between those two layers is where the atmosphere lives. A site named by one group in one decade sounds like a label. A site named twice, by people who disagreed, sounds old.

Built as Used later as What the name keeps What that buys you at the table
Temple Sewer, then hiding place The god's name in the first element, a filthy word in the second The holy name is still on the door lintel while the street uses a joke. Players hear the insult and know the faith lost an argument.
Tomb Pilgrimage stop The dead person's name plus a visit word: saint, kneel, candle Locals light candles in the entrance hall and refuse to go past the second door. You get reverence and dread in the same room.
Mine Refuge, then abandoned The company name and a lot or shaft number Paperwork still exists. Claims are still valid. A deed found down there is a legal weapon, not a trinket.
Fortress Market, then ruin Garrison words frozen as room names The rooms still sound military while the town above uses trade names, so the site reads as an occupation the locals got over.
Granary or cellar Hermitage, then lair Words for volume: bushel, tub, measure, bin Room names that make no sense for worship tell observant players the truth: this place stored grain, so the floors are strong and the vents exist.
Natural cave Boundary marker, then oath site A law word: markstone, moot, oathhole The site is a legal document. Trespass has a penalty, and someone still enforces it.

The Reuse Test: Three Questions Before You Name It

Ask who maintained the site, who pays for it now, and who is embarrassed by it. If nobody maintained it, every name in use is a folk name and none of them are consistent. If somebody still pays for it, there is a register, a survey and a keeper with keys, and the official name will be the boring one. If somebody is embarrassed by it, the official name is a euphemism, and the euphemism is the clue: a site called "the Quiet Houses" is hiding something specific, and the party can find out what by asking who changed the sign.

Date the layers, not the site

Say how long each use lasted. A temple for four centuries with a sewer for twenty years reads very differently from the reverse, and the ratio decides which name wins.

One room keeps the oldest name

Let a single door, well or stair carry the builder's word while everything around it has been renamed. That room is now a plot hook with no rolling required.

Euphemism is a clue

A polite name on an impolite site means someone with money objects to the plain word. Name the objector, and you have named the antagonist.

The reuse explains the layout

Storage sites have wide stairs and small doors. Worship sites have processional axes. Whatever the site was, the architecture is still arguing for it.

Rule of thumb: name the site for its first use and the rooms for its last one. That mismatch is what makes a place feel excavated rather than designed, and it costs you one word per room.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I name a dungeon?

Decide who is doing the naming before you choose words. Neighbours name what they can see from the entrance, so they produce a condition plus a structure. Antiquaries name what they think it was for, so they produce a structure of an entity. A licensed company names ownership, so it produces a person and a number. Each choice also tells the players how much of the map to trust.

What is the difference between a crypt, a barrow and an ossuary?

A crypt is a stone chamber built to hold the dead, usually under or beside a building, so it implies architecture and a community above it. A barrow is a mound raised over a burial, which implies an older culture, an open field, and one grave rather than many. An ossuary holds relocated bones, which implies a decision was made to move the dead, often for space, often by people who were not the mourners. Each one changes what you find on the way in.

Should a dungeon have more than one name?

Yes. Give it a map label short enough to be drawn, a spoken name short enough to be shouted, and a record name long enough to be filed. The three forms disagree by design, and the disagreement tells players which kind of document they are holding and who benefits from that version.

How do I make a dungeon name frightening without sounding silly?

Describe an event instead of a mood. The Hungry Vault announces a feeling and invites laughter; Where the Lamp Went Out announces something that happened to a specific person and cannot be argued with. Equally, a boring administrative name over a terrible site is one of the strongest pairings available: a numbered lot with a fee schedule is scarcer sounding than a demon, and much stranger.

Can I use these dungeon names for my adventure or module?

Yes. Every output is an original combination of ordinary English nouns and invented names, so you can use them in tabletop sessions, published adventures, fiction, games, maps or streams without credit or payment.
