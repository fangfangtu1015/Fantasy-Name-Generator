A Guild Is a Monopoly, and Its Name Is the Invoice

A charter sheet for prep: both names, the sigil drawn in one line of blazon, the rank ladder, the dues, the gate the body actually controls, and the dispute the founding date implies.

Two Names, Used by Two Different Sets of People

This is the single thing that separates a guild name from every other name on this site: a guild always carries a legal name and a spoken name, and they are used by different people for different purposes. The charter name goes on a contract, a bond, a court filing or a toll board. The spoken name is what a worker uses, and what a threat uses. Getting both makes the organisation feel like it has paperwork, which is how you make an institution feel real faster than any description.

Form Typical shape Who uses it What it is for
Charter name Qualifiers, then the people, then the trade, then the place Clerks, magistrates, insurers, foreign buyers Binding money: the name a debt is registered under
Street name One word, or the trade plus a possessive Members, rivals, anyone arguing in a market Pointing: who to look for, who to complain to, who to avoid
Hall name The object, not the people: the Tallow Hall, the Long Counting House Everyone, when giving an address Location and status; the building is older than the charter
Exonym Whatever outsiders chose to call them, often insulting People the guild excludes Sometimes the only name the party has heard, which is itself a clue
The gap is the plot. When the charter name and the street name disagree, ask who benefits from the difference. A body chartered as "the Company of Carriers" and known as "the River Men" is claiming a water monopoly it has never been granted in writing, and that single mismatch is an adventure without any monsters in it.

Eight Forms, and Each One's Name Makes a Different Promise

Players who have run games for years react to the form word before they react to the trade word. "Company" means you can buy your way in and the door is commercial. "Order" means vows, and therefore a rule that will cost a character something. "Circle" means the membership is small and knows each other by face, which reads as arcane or as conspiracy. Choose the form deliberately: it is a promise about how the party can get inside.

Form Membership logic What the name promises What the name hides
Company Shares, admission fees, hired staff You can be bought, and so can they Who actually holds the shares
Guild or mystery Apprenticeship, then a masterpiece, then a seat Quality control and a closed trade How many members are too old to work
Order Vows, obedience, houses in different cities Discipline, and money that is not anyone's That the rule applies to the rich differently
League Separate bodies pooled for one purpose A common enemy or a common toll That it dissolves the moment the purpose ends
Circle Nominated, capped, unlisted Access through a person, not a fee What happens when one member dies
Brotherhood Sworn equals, often with a shared grave or fund Loyalty stronger than a contract That the fund is the real product
Wardens Office holders elected annually by a group that cannot meet A duty over a place or a thing That the office outlasted the thing it guarded
Mask Whatever membership the front can prove on paper A respectability it is trading for Everything, deliberately, in plain sight

Four Ways a Guild Acquires Its Name

Real trade bodies name themselves by four mechanisms, and each one tells you something the party can use before they walk in.

Pattern How it forms What it implies about the trade
Trade plus people word Chandlers, Ferriers, Weavers, or a verb turned into a noun The product is the identity; quality is enforceable and marked
Patron or observance Named for a venerated figure, a chapel, or a burial custom The guild doubles as a funeral club and a mutual fund; leaving is socially expensive
Sigil first Called after the mark on their goods: the Three Shuttles, the Black Hook The stamp matters more than the maker, so the guild is really a standards authority
Number or sequence the Second, Nine Houses, Lot and Ward numbering, the Sixth Company A merger, a split, or a body that has been re-founded after being banned once

Numbers Lie Beautifully

A numbering pattern is the cheapest institution-building device available. "The Second Chandlers' Company" states, without any exposition, that the first one ended badly; that somebody kept the name; and that the current holders are worried about the difference. Write the founding date on the charter and you have created a dispute for free: dates that are too recent for the trade mean someone bought the right to say they are old.

Criminal Guilds Deliberately Borrow Civic Vocabulary

Illegal bodies almost never call themselves anything menacing, because menacing names attract the watch. They take the vocabulary of legitimate mutual aid and bury themselves in it. A "Porters' Friendly Society" holding a weekly meeting of forty men is invisible; the same forty men meeting as "the Black Hand" is a raid within a month. If you write your thieves' guild with a boring, charitable-sounding name, you get realism and a much better reveal.

Front name What is filed What actually moves How the party finds out
Friendly societies and burial clubs Funeral subscriptions for dock labour Enforced dues, protection on the wharf Widows being paid on time, and nobody asking whose money
Secondhand and salvage trade Buying damaged goods at auction Fencing; the ledger lists one item twice A stolen object appearing legitimately, at the same price
Porters, messengers and carriers Human carriage of parcels Drugs, letters, small stolen goods, information A courier who arrives before the letter he is carrying
Lamplighters, sweeps, water carriers Municipal service by contract Access to every building in the ward, and a map of who is in Burglaries only in streets that were serviced that week
Alms, charity and hospital support Gifts and legacies Laundering, and a list of everyone who owes a favour A donation the size of the crime that produced it

Keep the register boring

The more frightening the operation, the duller the name should be. "The Widows' Almshouse Committee" is a hundred times worse in play than a name with teeth in it.

One honest name each

Give a front body exactly one internal name the members use, and keep it short. That is the moment the party learns the two-layer structure, and it lands better than a monologue.

Make the paperwork visible

Let them find the licence, the bond, or the subscription book rather than the weapon. Institutional evidence reads as bigger than the room it sits in.

Price the betrayal

Every guild name is worth something to somebody. Decide what it costs to be expelled, and you have decided how badly a member will lie to protect the body.

Ranks and Dues: Why the Name Matters to Anyone

Nobody inside a guild cares about its name; they care about what the name lets it do to their income. Put a ladder and a fee in your notes and the institution becomes concrete in one sentence.

Rank Addressed as What the body controls over them Entry cost
Apprentice By the master's name, not their own Where they live, who they may marry, how long A premium paid by the family, and years of labour
Journeyman By name, no title Whether they may take work in this ward at all A piece of work judged by three masters
Master or Fellow Master, or Goodman The right to trade, to vote, and to be tried by peers A masterpiece, a fee, and a banquet nobody can afford
Warden or Deacon Sir, or by office Inspection, seizures, and the guild's key Election by the full body, usually unpaid and unwanted
Clerk or Assayer By office, always The records, which means the truth Appointment, and a lifetime of being distrusted
Factor or Agent Not at all, in public Nothing, and knows where everything is Hired at distance, paid a percentage

The reason this is worth two minutes of preparation: a player who is told "you may not sell that here without a fellow's ticket" instantly understands the city better than any amount of describing buildings. The guild name is what they will use to negotiate with that rule, which is the moment your naming work pays off.

Four Instruments, and the Name Is Only One of Them

A guild's power is exercised through four physical objects, and each one carries a different version of the body's name. That is useful because it lets you communicate an institution's reach through inventory: what the party is shown, and what they are asked to produce. Once a player has been fined for a missing ticket, they understand the guild better than any description of its offices could teach them.

Instrument What it does Which name it carries What it is worth to the party
The mark Struck into or painted on goods to certify who made them The sigil, and nothing else; no words, so it works on a foreign quay A blank one is a criminal business; an old one in a cellar is a whole adventure
The seal Closes a document and makes it enforceable against anybody The full charter name, in a ring of letters Whoever holds it can bind the body, whether or not the body agreed
The ticket Records that a fee was paid for a specific load, gate or shift The hall name and a number, written in the clerk's hand Forged by the thousand; the cheapest forgery in the city and the easiest to check
The search The right to enter a workshop, inspect and seize No name at all; two wardens and a witness is the whole authority An unaccompanied search is void, which means a witness is worth rescuing

What the Party Can Forge, Steal or Buy

Set the difficulty of each instrument by what it proves. A ticket proves a payment and is worthless without the counterfoil, so it is easy to copy and hard to use. A mark proves workmanship and travels, which makes a stolen mark the most valuable forgery in the setting and the one the guild will pursue personally rather than through the watch. A seal proves consent, so stealing it does not defraud anyone: it binds the whole body to whatever the thief wrote, which is why the seal is kept by three people who dislike each other. Once the party understands that the least impressive object in the room is the one with real authority, they have learned your city's actual power structure without a lecture.

Answer one question per guild and you can run it: which of these four instruments does it own outright, and which one does it have to borrow from somebody else? A body that must borrow its seal from a temple, or rent its mark from a richer company, is not as powerful as its charter name claims, and that gap is where an adventuring party fits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a guild, a company and an order?

A guild controls who is allowed to practise a trade, so its power is over its own members. A company exists to make money on a venture and admits anyone who buys a share, so its power is over a market. An order is held together by vows and obedience, which means it can own nothing individually and still outspend both of them. Pick the form first and the naming grammar follows.

How do I name a thieves' guild without it sounding like a joke?

Give it a charitable or municipal front name and keep the menacing version internal, if you use one at all. Real illegal bodies hide inside dull legitimate categories: burial clubs, porters' associations, salvage buyers, lamplighting contracts. "The Porters' Friendly Society" is far more frightening than an invented name with teeth, because it implies a membership list, dues, and a reason for forty people to meet weekly.

Should a guild name include the trade?

In the charter form, yes, because the whole purpose of the document is to state what the body claims a monopoly over. In the spoken form, usually no: members shorten to the sigil, the hall, the number or a single word. Writing both forms costs you nothing and makes the institution feel older than your notes.

How do I make a guild feel like it has real power in my city?

Attach it to one physical gate and one fee. Decide what cannot legally be sold, shipped, struck or buried without the body's mark on it, then set the price and the punishment. Power in fiction is not an army; it is the ability to make an ordinary transaction illegal on a Tuesday.

Can I use these guild names in my game or published module?

Yes. The generator combines ordinary historical trade words, invented figure names and heraldic phrasing, so results are free to use in tabletop sessions, published adventures, fiction, games, streams or videos with no credit and no fee.
