Names Built From Crossings, Camps and Quays

A city sheet: who founded it and why, what it is called on a map versus in speech, its wards and gates, market day, and the one thing it cannot feed itself.

A Settlement Is Named for What It Did Before It Was Famous

Old city names are almost never celebrations. They are invoices. A name records the reason a bunch of people stood in one muddy place: a crossing, a camp, a mill, a wall, a market day, a holy well, a seam in the rock. Once you know which fact a name is holding onto, you can generate settlement names that carry a plausible economic history, and you can read your own map for free: every functional suffix is a claim about where the water, the road or the tax came from.

Element Original fact What the city still is, structurally
-ton / -ham / -stead A farm, an enclosed home, a standing place Grew from one household. Expect an old core, a church or shrine at its centre, and narrow streets that were once field boundaries.
-bury / -borough / -berg A fortified place, a hill with a wall on it Defence drove the siting. The high ground is the prestige address and the walls are still property lines.
-caster / -chester A military camp with a grid and a road to it Planned inside. Straight axes, a walled rectangle, and a modern mess of alleys that grew outside it.
-ford / -bridge The place you cross, and whoever controls it charges Toll money built the town. Two halves on opposite banks, one bridge, and a permanent argument about which side pays.
-mouth / -strand / -wick River mouth, beach, or a trading and dairy place Water did the hiring. Warehouses nearest the quays, the wealthier houses uphill and away from the smell.
-by / -thorpe A settlement, and an outlying dependent hamlet Junior status is in the name. A "-thorpe" is a place that belongs to somebody else's larger town.
-mere / -pool / -well Standing water, or a water with a reputation Water was the reason and often the religion. Pilgrims, then markets, then a city that forgot why.
-field / -worth / -minter Open ground used regularly, an enclosed holding, a mint Periodic use, private holding, or state money. Fairground, estate, or treasury town.
Use it in play. When the party arrives, the founding fact is the first thing you describe and the first thing you can threaten. A ford can be flooded, a bridge can be taxed or burned, a camp can be excavated, a holy well can run dry, and a mine can be closed. Functional names hand you a plot instead of a postcard.

Layers: Who Renamed It, and What Survived

Most real cities are a negotiation between whoever arrived second and whoever was already there. The older layer rarely disappears; it moves. Conquest tends to replace the front half of a name and keep the back half, or replace the official name and leave the spoken one alone, so that a document and a dockworker describe the same place with different words.

Layer What it contributes Where it survives Story it implies
Oldest spoken layer Water, hill, wood, ford In the short name and in field names nobody official reads Nobody remembers what the first word means, and everybody uses it anyway
Imperial layer Camp, road, colony, garrison grid Street plan, city walls, anything measured The straight streets are somebody else's order, still obeyed
Faith layer A saint, a shrine, a relic, a well Wards, gates, market days and hospital names The calendar of the city was set by a building, not a ruler
Trade layer Exchange, strand, ware, tally, guildhall The newest and richest districts Money arrived late and immediately bought the good addresses
Founder layer A name, a vow, or a number Documents, coins, the seal on the gate Someone paid for this city and intends to be thanked for it

The Mismatch Is the Point

Give a city an official name from the newest layer and a spoken name from the oldest, and you get a place with a politics. Outsiders write the founding honourific on treaties; cart-loaders use a clipped version of something much older. Anyone who uses the wrong one in the wrong room has just revealed where they are from, which is the most useful thing a name can do at a table.

Planned Cities: Honour, Ideal, or Number

When a city is founded on purpose rather than grown, its naming register tells you who paid and how much they cared about being liked. Three registers cover almost every invented foundation, and they are easy to distinguish even before you know the history.

  • The honourific register. A ruler's name plus a land or city suffix, usually chosen by a court rather than by the residents. It sounds permanent and it is the first name changed after a revolution, which is why so many real cities have been renamed three times.
  • The ideal register. A virtue plus a settlement suffix: goodness, concord, union, providence, hope, plenty. Optimistic, plain-spoken, and slightly embarrassing to the second generation, who rename it to something shorter out of irritation.
  • The numeric register. Wards, lots, gates, streets or even whole towns named by order: Third Ward, Ninth Gate, Number Street. It implies a survey and a state, and it is the cheapest way to make a city feel administered rather than inherited.

Which Register Your Setting Can Support

A city founded by an empire gets a grid and an honourific name. A city founded by refugees gets an ideal name and no grid. A city founded by a guild gets a trade name and a ledger. If your city has none of these and is named for a person who was not a ruler, it grew around somebody's farm, and the "-ton" or "-ham" ending should be doing that work.

Naming Inside the City

The parts of a city follow four rules, and applying them consistently is what separates a city with three sentences of description from one the party can navigate.

Wards are named for what happened there

Tannage, salt, grain, iron, fish, wool, candles. A ward name is a job, and if the job is gone the name survives as an insult or a joke. Five wards gives you five industries and therefore five sources of tension.

Gates are named for the road they face

The gate takes the destination's name, not its own. That means a gate can be renamed when a trade route shifts, and that a party asking for "the North Gate" is asking the wrong question if the road moved.

Streets change when a guild falls

Streets carry the trade of the people who lived on them. When an industry is expelled, banned or bought out, the street name either freezes as a memorial or is renamed by whoever replaced it. Both are political acts.

Each district keeps one sound palette

Old quarters share old sounds; new quarters share flat, administrative ones. If every district in a city uses the same syllables, your map is one neighbourhood wearing four hats.

Map Labels and the Two-Word Rule

Whatever the city is called in full, the map label must survive being written twice in ink at half an inch high. The practical limit is one word, two at most, and the longer civic name belongs in the journal, not on the parchment. If your generated name needs three elements to work, shorten it before you draw the map, then let the archive keep the long form.

Eight Kinds of City, and Each One Names Things Differently

The single fastest way to make a fantasy city feel specific is to decide which kind of city it is, because the type determines who has the authority to name anything. A university town names its halls after founders and its alleys after dropped exams. A garrison names everything after roads and captains. Choose the type and the vocabulary arrives with it, including the parts you will not have to invent.

City type Its native vocabulary Who gets things named First thing a visitor notices
Episcopal seat Close, minster, almonry, vestry, sanctum, bell, cross A chapter that has been here 200 years and fears nobody Two clocks disagreeing, and every second building has a gate
Garrison town Barrier, mount, parade, store, drawbridge, quarter An officer whose name is a street and a debt Prices double inside the walls and soldiers are served first
Deep-water port Quay, key, strand, wharf, lighters, bond, slip A customs house and the tide, in that order Goods exist here before they are legally owned anywhere
Mining boom town Shaft, adit, level, dump, flume, claim, pit Whoever holds the water right, this season Streets laid out for a city of fifty thousand that is currently ten
University town Hall, college, close, lane, schoole, divinity, gate Founders, then donors, then the people the students mock Two laws in force and the town loses whichever one is tested
Free or charter city Hall, weigh house, tally, chamber, burg, franchise A council elected by the guilds, which means by money Nobody outside the walls can sell here, and everybody knows who is angry
Spa or pilgrimage city Well, bath, garden, walk, terrace, hostelry, shrine Whoever profits from the water, and whoever claims the saint Rents vary by season and the poor live in the flood plain out of sight
Citadel-subject city Fort, hill, upper, lower, gate, sally, ditch The commander, and always on the map's upper half One word, "up", does all the work in every direction given

Ask Who Has Authority to Name Anything

That question is the whole method. Name-giving authority is how a city's real power shows up in ordinary conversation, and it is cheaper to dramatise than any guard patrol. In an episcopal city a street cannot be renamed without the chapter's consent, so a new name is an act of defiance. In a boom town the name of a claim changes every season with the water right, so nobody remembers what anything is called. In a garrison the commander renames things for propaganda, badly and quickly, and the townspeople keep using the old name purely to be difficult. Pick one city, give one name to the wrong authority, and watch a session fill itself.

Mix two types, not one. Most memorable cities are a pair: a port that became a pilgrimage site, a garrison that became a university town, a mine that became a spa. Each half contributes its own vocabulary layer, and the argument between the two sets of names is the city's history told in street signs. The type table above is a menu for exactly that kind of combination.

Five Mouths, Five Names

Inside the walls a city argues about official names and spoken names. Outside the walls, nobody argues: each group that deals with the city has its own word for it, and none of those words were chosen by the city. This is free material, because an outsider name tells you who is speaking before they say anything else. Use it in the scene where the party hires a boat, asks for directions at a crossroads, or hands a letter to a clerk.

Who says it The name they use Why What it buys in a scene
Boatmen and porters A word for the landing, not the town: the Stairs, the Long Wharf, the Lower Stage They never see more than the water line, and their pay depends on which step they tie up at. A boatman who says "the Stairs" while your map says the city's formal name is telling you he has never been past the customs house.
Road traffic A distance word: the two-day town, the last milk, the toll end Travellers name places by what they cost, not by what they are. Every waystation conversation is a schedule. "Two days out" also tells the party how far help is.
The cathedral A saint word, usually older than the city's current name The church kept its own records through every conquest and never bothered to update them. A priest uses a name nobody else uses, and the party can date a document by which of the two the writer chose.
The tax office of a bigger neighbour A register form: the district name, a number, or a province word that swallows the city Assessment needs categories, and categories erase identity. The city does not exist in that paperwork. Finding your district in a foreign ledger is a whole session.
Upriver or rival quarter A slur, a joke, or the name the city had before it got rich Neighbours remember your old status and resent your new one. Using the wrong version starts a fight in a tavern with no dice involved.

Pick the Name by the Room You Are In

Decide which version the party hears in each scene and you have decided whose interests are in the room. A customs clerk uses the register form. A dock worker uses the water word. A pilgrim uses the saint word. A smuggler uses whichever one the others will not repeat in front of a guard. Once you have chosen four outsider names for one city, you can run a whole arrival without inventing anything: the party is handed a different name every time they ask a question, and the confusion is the lesson.

One outsider name per group

Do not let the same group use two forms. Consistency is what makes the pattern readable to players.

Distance names travel furthest

A road name can be understood three hundred miles away, which makes it the best word to put in a rumour.

Slurs have a date

Say when a mocking name was coined. Whoever remembers the reason is old, bitter, and useful.

Write the register name on the map

Maps are drawn by officials. Put the spoken and outsider names in your notes, where players have to earn them.

Quick test: if every character in your session calls the city the same thing, you have lost the cheapest worldbuilding on the page. Give the water, the road, the church and the tax office their own words, and let the party work out who is talking.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I name a fantasy city?

Start with the fact that made people stand there: a crossing, a camp, a mill, a wall, a market day, a well or a seam of ore. Attach the suffix that records that fact, then decide who arrived second and what they renamed. A name built this way carries an economy, a defence and a politics, so you can describe the city by reading its own name back to the players.

What do endings like -bury and -caster mean?

A fortified hill place and a military camp respectively, in the naming language that survived in Europe. In practice the difference is visible on the ground: a hill fort grows upward from a defended core with irregular alleys, while a camp city has a grid, straight axes and a wall rectangle that later builders pretended to respect. Other common endings record a farm, a ford, a bridge, a river mouth, a trading beach or an outlying dependent hamlet.

Should my city have both an official name and a spoken name?

Yes, unless the city is very new or very controlled. Real cities accumulate a formal name from whoever last paid for a document and a clipped spoken name from whoever last worked the docks. Writing both costs you one line of text and instantly tells players who is in the room when a particular form is used.

How do I name wards, gates and streets?

Use different systems for each, because they were named by different people. Wards take the trade that worked there. Gates take the destination the road leads to, not the district behind them. Streets take the trade or the building that dominated them, and get renamed when that trade is expelled. Keep one sound palette per district so an old quarter sounds older than a planned one.

Can I use these city names in my own project?

Yes. Every name is an original combination of invented elements and common settlement suffixes, and nothing here is a real place. Use them in campaigns, novels, games, maps or streams without credit or payment.
