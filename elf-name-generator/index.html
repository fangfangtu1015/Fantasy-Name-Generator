About Elf Names in D&D 5e

In the Player's Handbook (p.23), an elf typically claims adulthood around the age of 100 and can live to be 750, so an elven name carries the weight of a very long family history. The structure is simple: a given name plus a family or clan name.

Because elves outlive the other humanoids around them, two extra forms show up in the book. An elf may take a personal epithet to mark something they did, and they will often shorten their name when working among shorter-lived companions who cannot manage three Elvish syllables before breakfast.

Sample given names from the PHB elf tables include Aramil, Aust, Bryn, Erevan, Ivellios, Lia, Quarion, Soveliss, and Thamior. The book is explicit that these are tendencies rather than rules: "Little distinction exists between male names and female names."

Elf Family Names

The PHB gives elf family names in two forms: the Elvish original and a Common translation. A high elf is more likely to keep the Elvish form, while a wood elf dealing with non-elves will often use the translation instead. Because each surname is a compound of Elvish words, the Common version tells you what the name literally means.

  • Galanodel (Moonwhisper)
  • Holimion (Diamonddew)
  • Liadon (Silverfrond)
  • Meliamne (Oakenheel)
  • Amakiir (Gemflower)
Pick one list and stay inside it: Ivellios Galanodel reads as an elf who kept the Elvish surname, while Ivellios Moonwhisper reads as the same family name translated for human ears. Choosing between them is a worldbuilding decision, not a spelling one.

Elf Subrace Naming Differences

The PHB describes three main elven subraces -- high elves, wood elves and dark elves -- and each has its own naming tendencies.

High Elf

High elves come from ancient, scholarly civilizations, so their names tend to run longer and more formal, and they keep the Elvish form of the family name. Suitable for a high elf wizard or noble:

Ivellios Galanodel, Quarion Holimion

Wood Elf

Wood elves live closer to nature and use the Common translation of the family name when dealing with non-elves -- Moonwhisper rather than Galanodel, Oakenheel rather than Meliamne. Given names skew shorter:

Aust, Bryn, Lia, Soveliss

Drow (Dark Elf)

Drow names use harsher consonants (X, Z, V) and apostrophes that mark glottal stops. Drow society is matriarchal, so female names run longer and more elaborate than male ones. For the full convention, including noble house names, see the Drow Name Generator; the detailed treatment is in Mordenkainen's Tome of Foes.

Tips for Choosing an Elf Name

  • Match the subrace to the character. A wood elf ranger and a high elf wizard follow different naming tendencies in the PHB, and the name is the fastest way to signal which one you are playing.
  • Keep it pronounceable. You will say this name dozens of times at one session. If the table stumbles on it, the table will rename your character within a week.
  • Decide on the Common translation. For a wood elf who trades and travels with non-elves, the translated surname is the more believable choice.
  • Generate several and pick one. The name that survives contact with your character's backstory and personality is the right one, not the first one that looked pretty.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are some official D&D 5e elf names?

The Player's Handbook (p.23) lists elf given names including Aramil, Aust, Bryn, Erevan, Ivellios, Lia, Quarion, Soveliss, and Thamior, with family names such as Galanodel (Moonwhisper) and Holimion (Diamonddew).

What's the difference between high elf and wood elf names?

Both use the same given name plus family name structure from the PHB. High elves tend to keep the Elvish form of the family name, while wood elves often use the Common translation -- Moonwhisper instead of Galanodel.

How do drow names differ from other elf names?

Drow names use X, Z and V consonants with apostrophes marking glottal stops, and follow a matriarchal structure in which female names are longer. See the Drow Name Generator for the full conventions.
