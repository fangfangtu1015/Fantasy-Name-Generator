The Real Problem Is Twelve Names in Six Seconds

A roster for one settlement: a single palette, no two people sharing an initial and a syllable count, the trades the party will actually go looking for, and the family scaffolds that turn three names into a street.

Three Tiers of NPC, and What Each Name Has to Carry

Most naming advice treats all non-player characters as the same job. They are not, and the difference is measurable: how many times you will say the name out loud. That single number decides how much syllable the name can afford, whether it needs a surname, and whether a nickname is doing the work instead.

Tier Times said aloud What the name must carry Typical shape Failure mode
Walk-on Once, maybe twice in a scene Nothing but a human being standing there One given name, two syllables at most A grand name that the party tries to remember, then never does
Working Ten to thirty times this session A cue for what this person does and who they answer to Given name plus occupational or locative surname Two working NPCs with the same first letter and the same rhythm
Recurring Every session for a year Relationship, history and a hook in the name itself Given name, surname, nickname or byname, relation A name that cannot be shortened, so players invent one for you
Spend the name where it earns rent. A walk-on carrying a four-syllable name with a compound surname steals attention from the recurring character sitting next to them. The most common table mistake with NPC names is not a bad name; it is a name with the wrong weight class.

Occupational Surnames Do Half Your Prep

English surnames are mostly frozen job descriptions, and that is directly useful at a table. If the blacksmith is Hob Bellows and the tanner is Ness Fothergill, the party can find the right person by asking for a trade rather than remembering your notes. It also gives you free competence: a player who asks "who else works leather around here?" gets an answer from the naming system itself.

Trade Surname you can use Who complains to them The smell or sound that sells it
Fuller (cleans and thickens cloth) Tucker, Fuller, Walker Weavers, dyers, anyone whose cloth shrank Stale urine and hot water; the building is always damp
Tanner (cures hides) Tanner, Barker, Fothergill Cobblers, saddlers, the neighbourhood downstream Dog-tight stench; tanners sit at the far end of town, always
Cooper (makes vessels) Cooper, Hoop, Barrelman Brewers, salters, anyone moving liquid or grain Rhythmic tapping at every hour; shavings in the gutter
Wright (builds and repairs generally) Wright, Arkwright, Joiner Carters, farmers, the shipyard, the mill Green timber smell; a yard full of half-finished things
Chandler (makes and sells candles, soap, tallow) Chandler, Licht, Tallow Temples, taverns, every household in winter Greasy smoke and a sweetness that clings to your sleeve
Carter (hauls by road) Carter, Haulk, Wheelwright Anyone whose goods missed the season Cracked wheels, loud opinions about road surfaces
Miller (grinds grain, controls a water right) Miller, Milburn, Thresh Every farmer in the valley, and the miller's own accountant Flour dust in every seam of clothing; a miller is never poor
Ferrier and farrier (shoes horses) Forrer, Ferrier, Shoe Cavalry, carters, anyone who buys a horse too cheaply Scorch of hot iron on wet hoof; horses calm down when they smell it

The Hook Hidden in Frozen Surnames

Because these names are inherited rather than chosen, they lie. A Cooper who has never made a barrel, a Tanner family now too respectable to touch hides, a Miller who lost the water right three generations ago: all of those are one sentence of backstory, and they arrive for free the moment you use a real occupational surname. This is why the form is worth more than a decorative fantasy last name.

One Village, One Sound Palette, Eight Distinguishable Names

A settlement feels real when its names share a sound palette, and it feels like a bad session when its names share a shape. Both are cheap to control. Pick one palette per place, then break the names apart by initial letter, by syllable count, and by which syllable is stressed. That is the whole technique.

Palette Sound rules Sample given names What breaks it
Market town One or two syllables, blunt endings, no apostrophes Hob, Nell, Brock, Maud, Tib, Garret Anything with an elision or a double vowel
Coastal Liquids and nasals, open final vowels Lina, Nello, Alis, Solenne, Illo Hard stops like k, tch, dg
Border march Initial k and g, clipped final consonants Kessa, Gorrit, Brokk, Tennek, Olka Names of three or more syllables
Old city Three or four syllables, internal vowels, family names first Aurelia Ventin, Sabba Orine, Casilane Monosyllables, which read as servants or outsiders
Highland Aspirates, broad vowels, names that end in a breath Hamha, Rhua, Eocaan, Ulaith Latinate endings and diminutive suffixes
River trade Shortened, mixed origins, jobs used as names Two-Ropes, Sable, Half-Pell, Clerk Full formal names, which mark you as new on the water

Initials audit

Before a session, check the first letters of everyone you plan to voice. Two Bs or two Ms in one scene is where players start saying "the other one", which is how an NPC stops existing.

The shout test

Say the name across a table while somebody else is talking. If you cannot land it, it is a walk-on name doing recurring work. Shorten it now and write down the short form.

Spelling trap

Players will ask how a name is said roughly once in five. Keep one pronunciation note per unusual name in your roster; the alternative is improvising it differently each time and losing the character.

Reuse trap

Never hand a walk-on the name you were saving for the villain. Reusing a strong name later makes the new character feel like a retread, and players who remember the first one will tell you so.

Scaffolds: One Name Becomes a Family, Then a Street

The cheapest way to populate a village is not more names. It is relations attached to names you already have, because each scaffold answers a question the party was about to ask.

Scaffold Example What it implies without a word of exposition
Patronymic Hobb the Younger, son of Hob Bellows A trade is held in a family; two people share a shop and a grudge
Locative Nell at the Ford, Tib behind the Mill Where the party finds them, and that nobody has house numbers here
Marital or widow form Maud, Bellows' widow Who runs the shop now, and who resents it
Comparative byname Tennek the Less, Brokk Twice A naming collision happened and the village solved it rudely
Office as name The Bailiff, Clerk, Watchman Perris Authority is smaller than the office holding it
Reputation phrase Garret-Who-Paid, Sable Nine-Fingers One past event defines a person permanently in a small town

Run three names and three scaffolds and you have nine people, all of them related to each other, none of them requiring preparation. Related NPCs are also how you make a village feel older than the session: "no, that's the other Miller, the one who lost the water right" costs you one sentence.

Six Names in One Scene, Said Out Loud, Under Pressure

Naming is the easy half. The hard half is producing the name verbally while a player is talking at you, and holding six of them distinct across a twenty-minute scene. Working memory at a table carries about four names reliably; the fifth is where players start saying "the innkeeper", which is how a prepared NPC disappears. Everything below exists to keep the count down and the distinctness up.

Moment at the table What you actually have to produce Technique that works Example
Someone walks in and asks a question One name, said once Take a walk-on from the palette; never invent on the spot "Nell. Nell wants to know who broke the gate."
Someone bargains or is threatened A name the party will reuse within ten minutes Use the surname as the handle, since it is shorter and means something "Tell the Tucker that the cloth is not leaving the ward unpaid for."
The same person returns next session A name plus a relationship Attach one scaffold and it stops being a new person "Hobb the Younger. Son of the Bellows. Same shop."
Three people in one room Three names that never sound alike Vary the initial and the syllable count; two-syllable, then one, then three Brock, Nell, Aurelia
A crowd, a queue, a market No names at all Trades only, in the plural, which reads as bigger than any roster "Two coopers and a carrier are already arguing about the loading order."
A name the players mishear A correction that does not embarrass them Have the short form ready; players always prefer the shorter one "Aurelia Ventin. Everyone says Venn."

Three Emergency Methods When Nobody Is Named Yet

Keep these in reserve for the moment a player asks about the person you did not prepare. First, name by trade with no given name at all: "the Ferrier", "the Clerk". It is honest, it takes one second, and it is a better clue than a fantasy word, because a trade tells the party where to find that person again. Second, derive from the palette you are already using rather than from nothing: take a name you have said already, keep its first syllable and change the ending, and you have produced a sibling, a cousin or a rival with an audible family resemblance. Third, borrow a real surname off any object in your room and adjust one letter; nobody at the table can tell, and the shape of a genuine surname is exactly what invented ones usually fail to imitate.

One physical detail per name

Players recall a name when it is stapled to something: a sleeve, a limp, a smell, an habit of speech. One detail buys you three sessions of recall on a name you spent no preparation on.

Give the palette a voice, not each NPC

Slow the rate for long latinate names, clip the march names, and keep everything else at normal speed. One rule per settlement is sustainable; one rule per character is not.

Write names the players will not have to read

If a name only exists in your notes, it does not matter how it is spelled. If it goes on a handout, it must be spellable after one hearing, or you will be spelling it out for the rest of the campaign.

Retire names on purpose

When a walk-on unexpectedly matters, rename them properly and say the new form twice. Players accept it, and you keep your strong names for the characters who earned them.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I name an NPC quickly at the table?

Pick the tier first, not the name. If you will say it once, take any two-syllable name from a single palette and move on; if you will say it thirty times, attach a trade to it, because the trade makes the name recallable. Having a rule for tiers removes the hesitation more effectively than having a good list of names.

Do NPC names have to match the character's race?

No, and the table will not check. What the table does check is pronounceability and distinctness. Use the race generators when ancestry is part of the character's role in the scene, and otherwise favour a settlement palette, since people who live together sound like they live together.

Should NPCs have last names?

Only working and recurring NPCs. A surname on a walk-on is dead preparation, and a surname on a recurring character is a promise you should keep. When you do use one, prefer an occupational or locative form, because those carry information the party can act on.

How many NPCs does a village need?

Six to eight named, plus a handful of roles that only ever get an office. That is enough for any party to bump into three of them by accident, and small enough that you can keep the initials distinct without a spreadsheet. More than ten named people in one settlement is a roster problem, not a flavour one.

Can I use these NPC names in my game or published module?

Yes. The output combines ordinary historical English surnames with invented given names, so you may use them in tabletop sessions, published adventures, fiction, streams or games with no credit and no fee.
