Dark Elf Names for Your D&D Campaign

Dark Elf Naming: What Makes a Name Work?

Dark elf names appear across many fantasy settings — D&D's drow, the Dunmer of The Elder Scrolls, the Dark Elves of Warhammer, and the Dökkálfar of Norse mythology. While each setting has its own conventions, dark elf names share some common traits:

  • Harsh consonants: X, Z, V, and Q sounds appear frequently, giving names an alien or dangerous quality
  • Apostrophes: Many dark elf names use apostrophes (Do'Urden, Bregan D'aerthe) as a visual marker of exotic origin
  • House or family names: Dark elf societies are often organized into houses, clans, or families, and these names carry political weight

Dark elf vs. Drow in D&D: "Drow" is the specific D&D term for dark elves — an elf subrace (PHB p.23) from the Underdark. In the Forgotten Realms, many drow societies are strongly influenced by Lolth worship and Underdark politics. However, drow characters can follow different beliefs, reject traditional social structures, or build lives beyond the Underdark. If you're building a D&D character, see our Drow Name Generator for official D&D naming sources. This page covers dark elf names more broadly, for any fantasy setting.

On Norse mythology: Norse mythology also includes the Dökkálfar, or Dark Elves, described in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda. Their identity and relationship to other underground beings in Norse mythology are debated, so they should not be treated as a direct equivalent of modern fantasy drow.

Choose a Naming Style

Four creative directions for dark elf names. These are starting points, not generator modes — use them to guide your choices.

Noble

A dark elf with political power, using a full name with House or family affiliation. The name signals rank and allegiance.

Example: Malice Do'Urden (D&D), Malekith (Warhammer)

Underdark

A common Underdark resident who is not noble. Has a personal name, may have a family name, but lacks the political weight of a House name.

Example: Jarlaxle (D&D — born into House Baenre, but established Bregan D'aerthe outside the traditional House hierarchy)

Exile

A dark elf driven out or escaped from their society. May have abandoned their House name, or kept it as a reminder of what they lost.

Example: Drizzt Do'Urden (D&D — an exile who kept his House name but rejected its values)

Independent

A dark elf whose identity is defined by personal power or choice, not by house affiliation. This doesn't mean they have no house — it means the house is not the primary lens through which they are known.

Example: Neloth (Elder Scrolls — a Telvanni wizard whose personal reputation outweighs his house affiliation)

First Names, House Names, and Titles

Dark elf names can include several components:

  • First name: The personal name given at birth or chosen later
  • House / family name: The political or familial affiliation (Do'Urden, Baenre, Xorlarrin). Not all dark elves have one
  • Clan or sub-house name: A subdivision within a larger house — less common, mainly in D&D lore
  • Title: An earned or inherited descriptor (Matron Mother, Weapon Master, Archmage). Titles go before the name in formal address
  • Alias: A name used on the surface or in exile, often simpler than the Underdark name

Not every dark elf character needs all of these. A simple first name is enough for many characters. House names and titles add flavor when the character's political background matters.

Build Names for an Entire Character Group

When you need names for a family, faction, or adventuring party — not just one character — consistency matters more than any single name.

For a drow House (D&D):

Pick a House name with apostrophes or harsh consonants (Xorlarrin, Freth, Kenafin). Give all members the same House name. Vary first names by role: shorter names for warriors, longer names for priestesses. Example House:

  • Matron Zilvra Xorlarrin — house matron
  • Vierna Xorlarrin — eldest daughter, priestess
  • Nalfein Xorlarrin — weapon master
  • Ryvel Xorlarrin — youngest son

For a Dunmer council (Elder Scrolls style):

Use Telvanni-style names: short, unusual, often without apostrophes. Example council:

  • Neloth — council elder
  • Divayth Fyr — ancient wizard
  • Therana — reclusive mage
  • Aryon — junior councilor

For a surface dark elf party:

Mix naming styles to reflect varied backgrounds. One character may have a full House name, another may use only a first name, another may have adopted a surface alias. The inconsistency is the point — it tells a story of where each character came from.

How to Choose and Refine Your Generated Name

  1. Say it aloud. If you can't pronounce it quickly at the table, it won't work. Apostrophes don't have to be glottal stops — treat them as syllable breaks if that's easier.
  2. Check for duplicates in your party. Dark elf names with similar sounds can cause confusion.
  3. Decide on a short form. Long names get shortened in play. Malice Do'Urden becomes Malice. Make sure the short form works.
  4. Match the name to the character's story. A noble needs a House name. An exile may have dropped theirs. A surface dweller may use an alias.
  5. Consider your setting. D&D drow names use apostrophes and House names. Elder Scrolls Dunmer names often don't. Warhammer dark elf names are harsh but varied. Pick the convention that fits your game.
  6. Build from sound, not from spelling tweaks. If you like the feel of a published name, extract its sound profile — syllable count, key consonants, ending — and build a new name from those elements. Changing one letter of a famous name is not original naming.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a dark elf and a drow?

In D&D, "drow" is the specific term for dark elves — an elf subrace (PHB p.23) from the Underdark. "Dark elf" is a broader term used across many fantasy settings, including the Dunmer of The Elder Scrolls, the Dark Elves of Warhammer, and the Dökkálfar of Norse mythology. This generator covers dark elf names broadly; for D&D-specific drow names, see the Drow Name Generator.

Do dark elf names need apostrophes?

No. Apostrophes are common in D&D drow names (Do'Urden, Bregan D'aerthe) but not universal. Elder Scrolls Dunmer names (Neloth, Divayth Fyr) and Warhammer dark elf names (Malekith, Morathi) typically don't use them. Use apostrophes if they fit your setting's convention.

Can I use names from other fantasy settings in my D&D game?

Yes, but be aware that players familiar with the source may recognize them. Malekith will read as Warhammer to some players. A better approach is to extract the sound profile and build a new name, rather than minor spelling changes to a famous name.
