About Dragon Names

This page covers two related but distinct needs:

  1. Dragonborn character names — names for player characters of the Dragonborn race.
  2. Dragon names — names for NPC dragons, boss encounters, or fictional world-building.

The Player's Handbook includes sample Dragonborn names, including personal names, childhood names, and clan names. Dragonborn typically place their clan name before their personal name as a mark of honor.

Sample Dragonborn names from the PHB:

  • Male: Arjhan, Balasar, Kriv, Rhogar
  • Female: Akra, Farideh, Nala, Surina
  • Childhood names: Climber, Leaper, Zealous
  • Clan names: Daardendrian, Kimbatuul, Turnuroth

For dragon names, Fizban's Treasury of Dragons includes a dedicated section on naming dragons, with a Dragon Name Elements table that provides Draconic name components readers can combine and adapt. It also offers guidance on dragon personalities and creating memorable nicknames or epithets.

Three Creative Directions

Naming an evil dragon

Evil dragons in D&D — particularly chromatic dragons — often have names that sound harsh and commanding. Look at published examples: Ashardalon (a red dragon from D&D history), Iymrith "The Sinister" (a blue dragon from Storm King's Thunder). The name doesn't need to "sound evil" through invented meaning — it needs to be memorable and carry weight. Short names with hard consonants (K, R, G, X) work well for antagonists the party will face repeatedly.

Naming an ancient guardian dragon

Ancient dragons who guard hoards or serve as set-piece encounters often have names that feel old and formal. Claugiyliamatar, also known as "Old Gnawbone" (a green dragon in Storm King's Thunder), and Arauthator, "Old White Death" (a white dragon in the same adventure) show two approaches: a long, elaborate name vs. a short name with an earned epithet. Either works — the choice depends on how the dragon fits into your world's history.

Does a dragon need a title or epithet?

Not always. Tiamat and Bahamut — the dragon deities in the Monster Manual — are known by single names. Many named dragons in published adventures use a name plus an epithet (Iymrith the Sinister, Arauthator "Old White Death", Claugiyliamatar "Old Gnawbone"). The epithet is usually earned through deeds, not inherited. If your dragon has a reputation, an epithet adds flavor; if the party is meeting this dragon for the first time, a name alone may be enough.

Dragon Type Naming

D&D 5e divides dragons into three families. These affect lore and alignment tendencies, not naming rules — there is no official "chromatic naming convention" vs. "metallic naming convention." Use these as creative starting points, not constraints:

  • Chromatic (black, blue, green, red, white): Traditionally evil in D&D. Published names tend toward harsh sounds, but this is a pattern, not a rule.
  • Metallic (brass, bronze, copper, gold, silver): Traditionally good. Published names are not systematically softer — Bahamut (platinum) is as hard-sounding as any chromatic name.
  • Gem (amethyst, crystal, emerald, sapphire, topaz): Introduced in Fizban's Treasury of Dragons. Neutral-aligned. No established naming pattern in published material.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there official D&D 5e dragon name tables?

Yes. The Player's Handbook includes sample Dragonborn names (personal, childhood, and clan names). Fizban's Treasury of Dragons (2022) includes a Dragon Name Elements table with Draconic name components for creating dragon names, along with dragon personality guidance.

What's the difference between a dragonborn name and a dragon name?

Dragonborn are a player race (PHB p.32). Their names draw from the Draconic language but follow personal and clan naming logic similar to other player races — clan name is placed before the personal name. Dragon names belong to the dragons themselves — ancient, powerful creatures with their own naming traditions. This generator covers both.

Do I need a title or epithet for my dragon?

No. Many named dragons in published D&D use a name alone (Tiamat, Bahamut). An epithet ("the Sinister," "Old White Death," "Old Gnawbone") is optional and usually reflects the dragon's reputation or deeds.

Can I use these names in my D&D campaign?

Names generated by this tool are original combinations. Named dragons from published D&D material (Tiamat, Bahamut, etc.) are official D&D content. For published or commercial work, check the Open Gaming License and System Reference Document to confirm what content is licensed for reuse.
